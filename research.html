01The question

When a curve feels wrong,
what exactly is wrong?

Zoom into the outline of a logo, a letter, or an icon: the anchor points look considered, the handles appear aligned, and yet the line seems to bulge by just a little. Manual correction often fixes one area while making another thinner, heavier, or less balanced. The hard part of Bézier editing is explaining what feels unnatural, preserving the designer’s intent, and changing only what is safe to change.

Our working thesisMeasure the discomfort.
Protect the intention.
02Continuity

Smoothness
is not one thing.

Two curves can meet at the same point and still create a visible bump. We study the levels of continuity that sit behind the word “smooth.”

Signed curvatureκ(t) = cross(B′(t), B″(t)) / |B′(t)|³

We compare how each side bends, including direction, as geometric continuity rather than raw second-derivative values.

03Fairness

A correct junction
is not a beautiful line.

Matching the two sides at the connection can still leave a swelling or a ripple somewhere along the curve. We separate local continuity from the fairness of the whole line.

Efair = ∫(dκ/ds)² ds

A representative measure for penalizing sudden curvature change. It is a guide, not a single answer: the smoothest curve may not be the curve that still feels like the brand.

05Perception

Not “0.01 units.”
How many pixels move?

The meaning of a coordinate difference depends on the final viewing size. A 0.1-unit change on a 1000-unit artboard is not a useful judgement until it becomes a visible distance.

09Conclusion

Do not decide the
right line for people.

harmonism line is not meant to replace the designer’s eye. It should make discomfort measurable, show what moved and what was protected, and make experimentation safe. The equations are only the starting point; the real research sits at the boundary between mathematics, perception, and interaction, without breaking the way Illustrator already works.
